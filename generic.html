Deciphering Big Data

This section presents my learning activities, collaborative discussions, practical work and reflections from the Deciphering Big Data module. The module explores big data technologies, data formats, data collection, storage, security, analysis and the management of complex datasets.

As the module progresses, I will add evidence from each unit, including reading summaries, discussion posts, practical activities, seminar preparation and reflective commentary.

Collaborative Discussion 1: The Data Collection Process

This collaborative discussion explored the rationale behind the Internet of Things (IoT) and critically considered the opportunities, limitations, risks and challenges associated with large-scale data collection. The discussion developed across the first three units through my initial post, responses to peers, feedback received and a final summary.

Initial Post

In my initial post, I explored the rationale behind the Internet of Things and considered how connected devices, such as wearable technologies, smart-home systems and industrial sensors, continuously collect and exchange data.

I identified real-time data collection as one of the main opportunities offered by IoT. In healthcare, wearable devices can support remote patient monitoring and potentially allow health problems to be identified earlier. In manufacturing, sensors can monitor equipment performance, predict failures and reduce unplanned downtime.

However, I also argued that collecting larger quantities of data does not automatically result in better outcomes. IoT systems can generate missing values, duplicate records and inconsistent information, which can reduce the reliability of subsequent analysis. This highlighted the importance of effective data cleaning and data management.

Privacy and cybersecurity were also important considerations. The increasing number of connected devices creates additional opportunities for cyberattacks and raises questions about how personal and sensitive information is collected, stored and used.

Overall, I concluded that the value of IoT depends not simply on the quantity of data collected, but on how effectively that data is cleaned, managed, governed and protected.

Responses to Peers

Responding to the contributions of my peers broadened my understanding of the risks and challenges associated with large-scale IoT data collection. The peer-review process encouraged me to move beyond identifying the consequences of poor data quality and security and consider how organisations could actively reduce these risks.

In my peer feedback, I considered preventative measures such as automated data validation, continuous monitoring of IoT devices, encryption, multi-factor authentication and regular firmware updates.

I also considered how AI-driven anomaly detection could help identify inaccurate or suspicious data before it enters the analytical process.

Data governance was another important area that emerged from my responses to peers. I considered whether governance frameworks and data-quality standards could provide organisations with clearer processes for maintaining the accuracy, security and reliability of information throughout the data lifecycle.

Engaging with the work of my peers therefore helped me consider practical controls that organisations can implement rather than focusing solely on the problems associated with IoT and large-scale data collection.

Feedback Received

The feedback I received on my initial post was positive about my discussion of the benefits of IoT, particularly its application in healthcare, and my consideration of data quality and data cleansing. However, it also identified several areas where I could develop my analysis further.

One important point was that traditional data-wrangling approaches may not always be sufficient for complex IoT datasets. Errors can accumulate over time, relate to other data and have several possible methods of correction, making data-quality assurance increasingly complex (Ding et al., 2022). This encouraged me to think beyond cleaning data after collection and consider more preventative and continuous approaches to maintaining data quality.

The feedback also encouraged me to examine the Mirai botnet in greater depth. Rather than viewing Mirai simply as an example of an IoT cyberattack, I recognised that it exposed wider technical and regulatory challenges associated with securing consumer-managed IoT devices (Antonakakis et al., 2017).

Finally, the feedback developed my thinking about data governance. I had initially considered governance mainly at an organisational level, whereas the feedback highlighted the importance of cooperation between governments and industry in developing adaptable, resilient and secure IoT environments (Trautman et al., 2020).

I incorporated these points into my final summary post, demonstrating how feedback from the collaborative discussion developed my understanding of IoT data quality, security and governance.

Summary Post

My initial discussion focused on the opportunities and challenges associated with the Internet of Things (IoT), particularly the importance of data quality when organisations collect large volumes of information. I initially considered IoT mainly in terms of its ability to generate real-time data that can improve decision-making, efficiency and automation. However, Huxley (2020) demonstrates that collecting more data does not necessarily produce better information, as the reliability of analysis depends on how effectively data is cleaned and managed.

The peer discussions broadened my understanding of these challenges. I initially focused on cleaning data after collection, but the discussions encouraged me to consider preventative approaches, including automated validation, continuous monitoring and anomaly detection (Mansouri et al., 2023).

I also recognised that traditional data-wrangling approaches may not always be sufficient for complex IoT datasets, particularly where errors accumulate over time or have several possible methods of correction (Ding et al., 2022).

The feedback also developed my understanding of IoT security and governance. I originally used the Mirai botnet to demonstrate the vulnerability of connected devices; however, further consideration showed how Mirai exposed wider challenges associated with securing consumer-managed IoT technologies (Antonakakis et al., 2017).

This reinforced the importance of encryption, authentication and monitoring, but also demonstrated that governance extends beyond individual organisations. Government and industry collaboration is important in developing adaptable and resilient IoT environments as technology and security threats continue to evolve (Trautman et al., 2020).

The first three units placed these issues within the wider context of big data. Unit 1 introduced volume, velocity, variety and veracity (Sardar and Pandey, 2024). Unit 2 developed my understanding of different data formats and APIs, while Unit 3 highlighted data collection, storage and integrity.

Overall, my understanding has developed from viewing IoT mainly as large-scale data collection to recognising that effective validation, cleaning, security and governance throughout the data lifecycle determine whether IoT data can be used reliably and responsibly.

Unit 2: Introduction to Data Types and Formats

Key areas covered

  • Data formats and representations
  • Structured and unstructured data
  • Semi-structured and quasi-structured data
  • Data types in programming languages
  • Python applications
  • Application Programming Interfaces

Reflection on Required and Recommended Reading

Completing the required and recommended reading for Unit 2 strengthened my understanding of the importance of selecting appropriate data types and formats for different analytical tasks. Sardar and Pandey (2024) highlighted how the way data is represented influences how effectively it can be stored, managed and processed within big data environments.

This helped me recognise that choosing an appropriate data format is not simply a technical decision, as it can also affect scalability, accessibility and the efficiency of data processing.

The reading also developed my understanding of the differences between structured, semi-structured, quasi-structured and unstructured data. I now have a greater appreciation of the range of information that organisations may need to manage and the fact that different types of data require different approaches to storage and analysis.

McKinney (2022) helped me connect these concepts with the practical use of Python. Chapters 3 and 6 developed my understanding of Python data structures and methods for working with information stored in different formats.

Overall, the reading increased my confidence in recognising different data formats and considering which format is most appropriate for a particular task. It also reinforced the importance of data quality, interoperability and effective data preparation.

Reading
